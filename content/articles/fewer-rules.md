Every mod team I've joined has had the same reflex. Something messy happens, someone in the mod chat says "we should have a rule for that", and by the end of the week the sidebar is one bullet point longer.

I've done it myself more times than I'd like to admit. I think it's one of the most common mistakes we make, and I'd like us to stop.

## Rules are for members, not for us

A rule's job is to tell members what the community expects of them. Most new rules aren't written for members, though. They're written so the mod team feels covered: so that next time, we can point at something.

You can usually spot these rules by their wording. "No posts that, in the opinion of the moderators, are low effort." Nobody reading that learns anything about what to post. It's a note to ourselves, pinned where everyone else has to read it.

## Every rule makes the others weaker

Members don't read rules the way we write them. They skim. A list of six rules gets read. A list of twenty-three gets scrolled past, and the important ones get lost among the ones about flair.

So each new rule has a cost, even if it's never enforced: it makes the rules that matter harder to find.

## What to do instead

When something goes wrong, I now ask three questions before anyone drafts anything.

1. **Is this already covered?** Usually it is, by "be civil" or "stay on topic". What's missing is a shared understanding among the mods of how to apply it. Fix that in your mod notes, not the sidebar.
2. **Will it happen again?** One bad weekend isn't a pattern. Wait and see.
3. **Can we explain it in one sentence a new member would understand?** If not, it isn't a rule yet. It's a judgement call, and that's what we're here for.

If a rule survives all three, write it. Then look for one you can delete.

## The objection I always hear

"Without a specific rule, people will argue every removal." Some will. They'll argue with a specific rule too: they'll just argue about the wording instead. A short, honest removal message does more to settle an argument than a rule written like a contract.

I'm not saying rules don't matter. I'm saying fewer, clearer rules, applied consistently, beat a long list applied whenever someone remembers. Next time your team reaches for a new one, try deleting one first.
